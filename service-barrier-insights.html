Summary

The problem

Governments needed to understand why services were failing. Teams researched it manually, but it took months, sometimes years, by which point the problem had often changed or grown.

What I did

Synthesised government reports, inquiries and research, compared a dashboard with an agentic workspace, and used a rubric to pivot to an AI agentic solution.

The outcome

A barrier-intelligence workspace that turns scattered signals into accountable action, with a department head validating every decision.

Overview

Closing the feedback loop in government services.

We saw this gap while building digital government services. Departments collect plenty of data, but often lack ground-level insight into where services actually break.

Frontline teams, help-centre agents, caseworkers, and community staff see these issues every day, because they meet residents and know exactly where they are facing these issues, but their feedback is often heard, logged, and forgotten.

Recurring barriers look like isolated cases. Governments miss patterns, systemic issues go unaddressed, and the same problems repeat across services.

This raised a bigger question: how might DGV turn the scattered evidence of resident struggle into something teams can learn from and act on?

The problem

Services are delivered. But the system doesn't learn from where they break down.

This problem has three faces, and we looked at it from all of them: the government running the services, the residents using them, and the frontline staff in between.

The government side

  • Departments track the number of applications and case outcomes, but lack visibility into why residents struggle or drop off.
  • The signals needed to improve the service are scattered across systems and teams.

The resident side

  • Residents struggle to find, understand, or complete a service.
  • Some abandon the process and others face preventable rejection.
  • A few speak up. Most say nothing and leave.

The frontline side

  • Frontline staff see these struggles but their systems only record numbers.
  • Nothing shows who never started, who gave up, or who was rejected for a preventable reason.
  • Their awareness rarely connects with operational data or reaches the teams who can change the service.
The result

The signals don't connect. Resident experience, frontline knowledge and operational data exist separately — so recurring barriers rarely become systemic problems to solve.

Consequences of ignoring the signals

The signals

A signal is any trace that shows a resident struggled — a call, search, abandoned application, rejection, caseworker note. Even silence is a signal.

Where they live

Signals are scattered across case records, calls, digital analytics, feedback, and community organisations. Some never reach the department at all.

Why they don't connect

Different teams own different channels, and each speaks a different language. No one sees the signals together.

What it costs

Residents repeat, appeal, or give up. Frontline teams solve the same problems repeatedly. The department sees individual cases instead of systemic barriers.

A LOT
Discovery

Before defining the product, I synthesised comparative evidence from government reports, public inquiries and academic research, focused on three questions:

01
Where do governments lose evidence of resident struggle?
02
Which signals reveal systemic barriers rather than individual cases?
03
What conditions would make a barrier-intelligence product safe and useful?

Four findings kept surfacing

The people most affected can be invisible in service data

Residents who never start, abandon midway, or rely on assisted support often disappear from completion and satisfaction metrics. A service can look successful while missing those who need it most.

Root causes are buried in operational data.

Frontline observations, rejection reasons, appeals and repeat contacts contain the “why” behind service failure, but are often treated as case-level records, not signals of systemic failure.

Barriers often span multiple channels

Someone may search online, struggle with an application, call for help, visit in person and still be rejected. Each channel captures one fragment of the journey, leaving no team with the full picture.

The full picture is spread across disconnected systems.

Signals sit across channels, legacy systems, and teams with different rules and structures. We don't need one record per resident; we need to connect patterns across signals to reveal recurring barriers.

Reframing the opportunity

Research showed that government already holds many signals of resident struggle like rejection reasons, abandoned journeys, repeat contacts, frontline observations and resident feedback. These signals are scattered and rarely lead to action. The opportunity was not another data-collection tool — it was a way to turn existing signals into service learning.

The deeper problem: building a barrier-intelligence system that brings evidence together, understands what it reveals, ensures the right team can act on it, and drives visible impact.

Process

The five-stage signal lifecycle.

Signal lifecycle
Each loop leaves the service a little better informed
1

Friction leaves a trace

A resident struggles to find, understand or complete a service. That experience leaves a trace: a failed upload, abandonment, contact, rejection or frontline observation.

2

Scattered traces become evidence

Signals from across channels are brought into view with their source, context and timeframe intact.

3

Evidence reveals a barrier

Related signals are synthesised into a recurring service barrier — not just a set of isolated operational events.

4

A barrier becomes an accountable decision

A department head reviews the evidence, assesses its severity and scope, and assigns responsibility for the next action.

5

Action becomes learning

The service tests an intervention and monitors whether the original signals reduce, creating evidence for the next decision.

The signals

To understand where services break down, the department needs four connected types of service signal.

01

Resident feedback

Residents share low-burden feedback at key moments — after applying, receiving a decision, abandoning a journey, or receiving support. This captures what was difficult, confusing, or unresolved in their own words.

02

Caseworker observations

Frontline teams capture barriers such as unclear eligibility, missing evidence, language needs, and digital exclusion, turning these insights into evidence that can be analysed across cases.

03

Rejection data

Every rejection becomes a barrier event. Records show the formal reason, whether the issue was correctable, and whether similar residents faced the same problem.

04

Invisible-population signals

Call-centre enquiries, abandoned journeys, website searches, assisted-digital demand, repeat visits without submission, and community referrals — showing who failed, left, never arrived, and where they were blocked.

Persona

As a department head, I want to see the full evidence behind recurring resident barriers, so that I can prioritise the right service changes and hold the right teams accountable for reducing harm.

Department head — Sarah Bennett
Responsible for service performance and improvement

Business needs

Bring together relevant service signals in one place, and observe patterns and create useful insights.
Help teams identify recurring problems in launched services faster.
Improve service quality by reducing avoidable rejection, abandonment, and repeat contact.
Help more eligible residents access and complete the service through the channel that works for them, not just online.

They do not use the platform to make decisions about individual residents. They use it to identify and reduce the systemic barriers that create those individual cases.

Define

Assumptions that helped while designing (LOFAs).

The beliefs the design is built on. If any of these are wrong, the core value proposition breaks down — so each one is something to validate, not assume.

01

Leaders need to understand barriers, not just outcomes

Leaders must value evidence of why residents fail, not only completion rates and throughput. Otherwise, barriers will not be reviewed — even when the evidence is visible.

02

Disconnected signals can reveal the same barrier

Rejections, calls, abandoned applications, and frontline observations can be connected into meaningful patterns without creating false correlations.

03

AI should recommend, not decide

When the system suggests a barrier, severity or owner, people must challenge and amend it when needed, not simply approve it.

04

Absence is also a signal

Non-applicants, drop-offs, callers and assisted journeys can reveal unmet need even when a completed application doesn't exist.

Ideal state

A service-learning system for government.

The goal is not to overload with every signal we can collect. It's a system that helps a department move from seeing what happened to knowing what to change — and then checking whether the change worked.

The department learning loop

01. Monitor: What is happening?

Track service data in real time: rejections, drop-offs, repeat contacts, complaints, caseworker flags, and access barriers.

02. Learn: What should we change based on the evidence?

Turn data and feedback into decisions, redesign actions, and institutional memory — so the organisation stops repeating the same failures.

03. Evaluate: Is the service working, and for whom?

Assess whether policy, process, or design changes are improving resident access, reducing barriers, and producing better outcomes.

The system that enables the loop

04. A capture layer brings signals into view.

Frontline caseworkers, residents, call-centre staff, and community partners contribute structured signals about barriers.

05. A learning outcome that connects the evidence.

It clusters similar problems, detects recurring barriers, summarises frontline observations, links rejection reasons to service-design failures, and drafts learning questions.

06. A decision workspace supports accountable action.

Department heads see barrier patterns, affected populations, service stages, responsible teams, severity, and whether action has been taken.

Success for the product

Helping governments identify where services fail, bring together the evidence to understand why, and make accountable improvements that reduce friction and help residents access the services and benefits they need.

A clear and structured view of barriers

Department heads can investigate barriers across available channels without manually chasing and reconciling evidence, while still seeing its source and limitations.

Faster, evidence-backed decisions

Less time is spent compiling feedback and creating insight; more time is spent prioritising the right service improvements.

More successful applications by the residents

Over time, relevant rejection, abandonment and repeat-contact rates reduce, alongside improved resident understanding and satisfaction.

Exploration

Exploring different design directions.

With a clear problem space and defined metrics, the next step was exploring what the platform experience could look like. Two directions were considered.

Direction A — Performance dashboard

Show the numbers.

A dashboard could show rejection rates, abandonment, service volumes, and emerging trends.

What it does well
  • Makes routine performance visible
  • Supports monitoring across services
  • Is familiar and relatively simple to adopt
Where it falls short

Sarah would still need to manually connect rejection data, feedback, support contacts, and frontline observations; judge whether a pattern is meaningful; and decide what should happen next.

Direction B — Chosen

AI agentic barrier intelligence workspace.

A workspace could start with a service-performance question, bring together available evidence across channels, surface a potential barrier, and prepare an accountable next step.

What it does well
  • Synthesises fragmented, cross-channel signals
  • Makes evidence sources and limitations visible
  • Helps identify recurring barriers, not just isolated metrics
  • Moves an investigation towards an owned action
Key insight

The problem was not a lack of dashboards. It was a lack of joined-up evidence and accountable follow-through.

We used a structured rubric to test the fit

We evaluated the opportunity, looking at the problem itself, the need for cross-system synthesis, and how the work should change.

Click to open full size

So, the conclusion: the complexity of the problem justified using AI agents. The system needs to combine fragmented signals, identify patterns across channels, and surface trends that would be difficult and time-consuming for a person to find manually.

However, it should not act autonomously. Decisions about service priorities, policy, and interventions can affect residents directly, and must account for incomplete evidence, equity implications, and public-sector accountability.

Turning point

From a performance dashboard to an agentic workspace.

Our research and the rubric test changed our direction towards an agentic solution — one that recommends, while people decide.

Final design

Creating accountable action from signals.

Fragmented signals→Surface various barriers→Barrier details with evidence→Accountable action (learning object)→Impact on service
Step 01 / Barrier list

Prioritise active barriers.

The starting point. Sarah sees a shared list of active barriers across services, ordered by priority, recency, and progress. She can move from a department-wide view to the specific barrier that needs investigation or ownership next.

Step 02 / Barrier detail

What is happening, and why we believe it.

Where Sarah goes when a potential barrier is surfaced, to decide whether the evidence warrants action and who should own the response. The screen answers four questions in order: what is happening, why we believe it, what it means, and what should happen next. She can stop reading at any point and still have a defensible explanation for her team.

Every insight is traceable to its sources.

Each agent insight links back to the underlying signals. Trust in AI-assisted analysis breaks the first time a reader cannot find the evidence behind a claim.

The agent's interpretation is separated from the data.

Prose insights are visually distinct from raw signal tables and charts, so Sarah can tell what the system inferred and what the underlying data shows.

What the screen deliberately does not do.

The screen never shows information about individual residents. It aggregates signals, identifies patterns, and proposes systemic change — keeping the focus on policy and service improvement, not automated decisions about people.

The hardest part was resisting the urge to make the agent look more decisive. Every element that looked like a definitive answer had to be softened into a proposal — because in government, unearned certainty is more dangerous than visible uncertainty.

Step 03 / Evidence view

Summary first. Source always.

Where Sarah inspects the underlying signals behind a barrier before deciding whether the pattern is supported enough to act on. The screen moves from the headline claim, to the evidence behind it, to the signals in reviewable form. She can pressure-test any part of the story by opening the relevant record — she never has to take the agent's word for it.

Evidence is layered, not summarised away.

The top strip surfaces the four strongest signals: rejections, upload stops, help-centre contacts, and repeat visits. Below it, supporting records, frontline observations, and resident feedback remain available. The agent's synthesis sits on top of the source, not in place of it.

Frontline and resident voices sit alongside operational data.

Rejection records show what happened. Caseworker observations and resident feedback add context about what it meant, so barriers aren't defined only by what the operational system happens to log.

The system explains the basis of its reasoning.

The 'What the evidence may indicate' section shows what each signal type contributed and where the confidence comes from. If Sarah disagrees, she can see which evidence to challenge.

Step 04 / Action creation

Turn a validated barrier into an owned action.

Sarah defines what needs to change, assigns the responsible teams, agrees how success will be measured, and commits the action for review.

The flow moves from what should change → who owns it → how success will be reviewed. Sarah can edit the proposed action before assigning it, so the system supports the decision without making it.

Beyond design

Designing for public-sector complexity.

AI assistance was justified by the complexity of cross-channel evidence synthesis — not because an agent was more novel than a dashboard. In government, useful AI must also be safe, governable, and worth its cost.

01

Public-sector trust and privacy are design constraints.

Government teams need to understand, challenge and override every recommendation. The system works with service-level patterns and minimum necessary data — not a unified resident record or automated decisions about individuals.

02

Our agentic solution had to justify its cost.

Agentic systems are more expensive than dashboards. They require secure integrations, evaluation, monitoring, audit trails, and ongoing human review. We chose AI assistance only because a dashboard would still leave department heads manually joining records, notes, feedback, and operational data.

03

Multiple bounded agents, one accountable decision-maker.

One agent standardises and classifies signals; another clusters related evidence and shows its sources; a third drafts possible actions and measures. The department head remains responsible for validating the barrier and taking decisions.

04

Human oversight is intentional.

We deliberately did not make the system fully autonomous. In public services, an incorrect priority or recommendation can have serious consequences for residents and be costly to correct. The system prepares evidence; the department head validates, prioritises and assigns.
